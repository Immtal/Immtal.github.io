0 Foreword

The volume is an important parameter when considering stellar dynamics.
The term “black hole” was first employed by John Archibald Wheeler (b. 1911).

1 Massive dark objects in galactic nuclei

The active galactic nucleus (AGN) can release energy and AGNs have large luminosities (10410^4104 times that of a typical galaxy) coming from tiby bolumes (≪1pc3\ll 1 \text{pc}^3≪1pc3). They are (one of) the most powerful objects in the universe.
Hertzsprung-Russell diagram (H-R diagram), is a scatter plot of stars showing the relationship between the stars’ absolute magnitudes or luminosities and their stellar classification or effective temperatures.
Accretion is a very efficient channel for turning matter into energy. Whilst nuclear fusion reaches only a few percent, accretion can transfer almost 50% of the mass-energy of a star into energy.
ANG has a broad frequency and radius ranges for emission, so they are far out of thermal equilibrium.

  • smaller regions are hotter;
  • components of utterly different temperature can exist together.

The release of gravitational binding energy by stellar accretion on to a MBH could be the primary powerhouse of an AGN. And MBH are found at the center of galaxies and acted in the past as the engines that powered ultraluminous quasars.
The central object is indeed a MBH rather than a cluster of smaller dark objects (like neutron stars, brown dwarfs etc), which has been probed only for two galaxies: the Milky Way and NGC4258.

Stars can be swallowed whole by MBH in two ways:

  • direct plunges
  • EMRI

Only when the compact object is dense enough will it not be dirupted by the tidal forces of the MBH. (like, a neutron star, a white dwarf or a small stellar-mass black hole.)
The significance of research on EMRI using GW: we can test the theory that massive dark objects are indeed Kerr black holes as GR predicts, and not exotic objects such as boson stars, which would be the ultimate test of GR (really!? cool!) The detection of such an EMRI will allow us to do very exciting science: EMRIs will give us measurements of the masses and spins of BHs to an accuracy which is beyond that of any other astrophysical technique. Such information will tell us about cosmic evolution, about the history and growth of MBHs in the nearby universe, with unprecedented accuracy.

2 GWs as a probe to stellar dynamics and the cosmic growth of SMBHs

It’s a statistical question of feeding a MBH, not a individual event question. And when we study GWs emitted by EMRIs, we have to study stellar dynamics as well:

  • two-body relation
  • many-body-coherent or non-coherent relaxation

Most nearby bright galaxies harbour a dark, massive, compact object at their center, and the correlation between the mass of SMBH and velocity dispersion of their host galaxies is shown before. Generally, $ M \uparrow$ and σ↑\sigma \uparrowσ↑.

Signifance: EMRIs are so appealing: a set of ∼ 10510^5105 bursts of GWs radiated by one system will tell us with the utmost accuracy about the system itself, it will test general relativity, it will tell us about the distribution of dark objects in galactic nuclei and globular clusters and, thus, we will have a new understanding of the physics of the process. New phenomena, unknown and unanticipated, are likely to be discovered. And on top of this, the measure of GWs will give us very detailed information about the spin of the central MBH, which is impossible with current techniques, X-ray observation of Fe Kα\alphaα profile, due to its uncertainties. Additionally, the secondary mass and the eccentricity of the orbit will be recovered from the GW with a tiny fractional accuracy (relative error). ( ←\leftarrow← which means high accuracy)
The mass range of central MBH in the search of EMRIs for LISA is between [107,10410^7, 10^4107,104] M⊙M_\odotM⊙​, (except the rotating MBH case, where the total mass can be to a few ∼107M⊙\sim 10^7 M_\odot∼107M⊙​), due to two reasons:

  • lareger than 107M⊙10^7 M_\odot107M⊙​: the frequency of the last stable orbit (LSO) is too low to detect.
  • less than 104M⊙10^4 M_\odot104M⊙​: the signal will be quite weak unless the source is very close.

3 A taxonomy of orbits in galactic nuclei

Since we are interested in EMRIs, we will focus on centrophilic orbits (中心亲和轨道) and leave centrophobic orbits (中心排斥轨道) aside.

4 Two-body relaxation in galactic nuclei

In general we refer ot “collisional” to any effect leading to exchange of energy and angular momentum among stars, sometimes we use “physical collisions” to refer to real collisions between two stars.

There are 3 ways of altering the angular momentum of stars:

  1. Two-body ralaxation It’s not the most relevant effect of contributing to EMRI sources, but it’s the best-studied effect.
  2. physical collision The impact of physical collision processes fro the global evolution of the dynamics of galactic nuclei is negligible. And the probability that stellar-mass black holes physically collide is negligible.
  3. seclar effects A keplerian orbit can be described in terms of its (1) semi-major axis and (2) eccentricity. The semi-major axis is only connected to energy and, for a given semi-major axis the eccentricity is connected to the angular momentum.

Three useful time-scales:

  1. The relaxation time We can regard it as the time interval required for the velocity distribution to reach the Maxwell-Boltzmann form.
    Relaxation can redistribute orbital energy amongst stelaar-mass objects until the most massive of them (presumably stellar-mass BH) form a power-law density cusp around the MBH, n(r)∝r−γn(r) \propto r^{-\gamma}n(r)∝r−γ. Nuclei likely to host MBHs in the LISA mass range (mass≤few×106M⊙\text{mass} \le \text{few} \times 10^6 M_\odotmass≤few×106M⊙​) probably have relaxation times comparable to or less than a Hubble time, so that it is expected that their heavier stars form a steep cusp, while less massive species arrange themselves into a shallower profile.
  2. Collision time tcollt_\text{coll}tcoll​ is defined as the required mean time for the unmber of stars within a volume to be one.
  3. The corssing time This is the required time for a star to pass through the system, i.e., to cross it.

Contrary to gas dynamics, trlx>tcrosst_\text{rlx} > t_\text{cross}trlx​>tcross​.
tdyn≪trlxt_\text{dyn} \ll t_\text{rlx}tdyn​≪trlx​, which means the system can get again a virial-type equilibrium in a short time, namely it changes in a quasi-stationary way.

Dynamical friction (DF) A star which is more massive than the average will suffer from dynamical friction, which is an effect of all encounters with lighter stars, namely relaxation boils down to dynamical friction in this case.

5 “Standard” mass segregation

Only a fool tries the harder problem when he does not understand the simplest special case.
Core collapse is not just a characteristic of multi-mass systems, but has been also observed in single mass analysis.
Mass segregation produces a core-collapse of the system that happens very fast.
During core collapse, the central regions of the cluster become completely dominated by the most massive stars. But, contrary to the case of single-mass clusters, the central velocity dispersion decreases.
The presence of a small fraction of stellar remnants may greatly affect the evolution of the cluster and growth of the MBH, because they segregate to the centre, and in doing so, they expel main sequence stars from it, but, being compact, they cannot be tidally disrupted.
In the cluster, the temperature of the inner part of the cluster will rise and the temperature of other all components decreases in the late.
EMRI emits GWs all the time, but the most important bursts of energy occur at periapsis. After every 2π2\pi2π around the orbit, the semi-major axis decays a fraction proportional to the energy loss. After typically some 104−10510^4 - 10^5104−105 cycles, the small body, the CO, plunges through the horizon of the MBH and is lost. The emission of GW finishes.
One usually assumes that the mass range of MBHs of intrest in the search of EMRIs for LISA is between [104,107]M⊙[10^4, 10^7]M_\odot[104,107]M⊙​. (The sensitivity limit for non-spinning black hole is about 5×106M⊙5\times 10^6 M_\odot5×106M⊙​, but this goes up to a few times 107M⊙10^7M_\odot107M⊙​ for prograde inspirals into rapidly spinning black holes.
) Because

  • If the central MBH >107M⊙> 10^7 M_\odot>107M⊙​, then the signal, even at the LSO, will have a frequency too low for detecting the system.
  • If the central MBH >104M⊙> 10^4 M_\odot>104M⊙​, the signal will be quite weak unless the source is very close.

post-Newtonian; nearly analytic

In principle (i.e., with sufficient computing power) the EMRI GW signals can be disentangled from the noise by matched filtering.

AK waveform include the effects of pericenter precession, Lense-Thirring precession, and inspiral from radiation reaction. AK neglects the spin of the second compact object. AK also

  • do not exhibit the extreme ``zoom-whirl’’ behavior of true fully-relativistic waveforms just prior to plunge
  • they approximate as constant the angle λ\lambdaλ between the CO’s orbital angular momentum and the MBH’s spin, whereas the correct evolution would show a small secular change in λ\lambdaλ.

Laser Interferometer Space Antenna LISA

Seismic noise is one of the main expereimental challenges for ground-based detectors, which limits their sensitivity at frequencies ≤10\le 10≤10 Hz, making them insensitive to GWs from MBH systems. However, space-borne interferometric GW detectors, such as LISA, are free from the seismic noise.
Stellar-mass BBHs are detectable by LISA years to days before they reach the sensitivity window of ground-based detectors, due to the different frequency band.

LISA Doppler phase modulation

LISA is orbiting around the Sun. For a distant plane wave, the arrival time at LISA differs from that at the barycenter by an additional geometric light-travel time

Refs:

  • Quasimonochromatic LISA sources in the frequency domain
  • LISA capture sources: Approximate waveforms, signal-to-noise ratios, and parameter estimation accuracy

Derivation:

Assuming the wave is exactly monochromatic, in the Solar barycenter system, the wave can be written as

h(t)=Acos⁡(2πf0t+ψ0)\begin{equation} h(t) = A \cos(2 \pi f_0 t + \psi_0) \end{equation} h(t)=Acos(2πf0​t+ψ0​)​​

where the f0f_0f0​ is a fixed frequency, and the ψ0\psi_0ψ0​ is the initial phase.

Assuming the relative position of LISA in the SBS is r⃗(t)\vec{r}(t)r(t), the unit vector of wave source is n^s\hat{n}_sn^s​. Thus the geometric time delay is

Δt(t)=r⃗(t)⋅n^sc=r⃗(t)⋅n^s (wiht c=1)\begin{equation} \Delta t (t) = \frac{\vec{r}(t) \cdot \hat{n}_s}{c} = \vec{r}(t) \cdot \hat{n}_s\, \text{(wiht $c = 1$)} \end{equation} Δt(t)=cr(t)⋅n^s​​=r(t)⋅n^s​(wiht c=1)​​

So, the phase becomes

ψ(t)=2πf0(t+Δt(t))+ψ0=2πf0t+ψ0+2πf0Δt(t)\begin{align} \psi(t) &= 2\pi f_0 (t + \Delta t(t)) + \psi_0 \\ &= 2\pi f_0 t + \psi_0 + 2 \pi f_0 \Delta t(t) \end{align} ψ(t)​=2πf0​(t+Δt(t))+ψ0​=2πf0​t+ψ0​+2πf0​Δt(t)​​

Therefore, the Doppler phase is ψD=2πf0Δt(t)\psi_D = 2 \pi f_0 \Delta t(t)ψD​=2πf0​Δt(t).

The radius of LISA’s orbit is approximately R=1AUR = 1 \mathrm{AU}R=1AU.
In the ecliptic（黄道） coordinate, LISA’s position can be written as

r⃗(t)=R(cos⁡ϕ(t),sin⁡ϕ(t),0)\begin{equation} \vec{r}(t) = R (\cos\phi(t), \sin\phi(t), 0) \end{equation} r(t)=R(cosϕ(t),sinϕ(t),0)​​

where ϕ(t)=ϕ0+2πt/T\phi(t) = \phi_0 + 2\pi t /Tϕ(t)=ϕ0​+2πt/T, T=1yrT = 1 \mathrm{yr}T=1yr。

In the ecliptic system, the coordinate of source is (θs,ϕs)(\theta_s, \phi_s)(θs​,ϕs​), the corresponding unit vector is

n^s=(sin⁡θscos⁡ϕs,sin⁡θssin⁡ϕs,cos⁡θs)\begin{equation} \hat{n}_s = (\sin \theta_s \cos\phi_s, \sin\theta_s \sin \phi_s, \cos\theta_s) \end{equation} n^s​=(sinθs​cosϕs​,sinθs​sinϕs​,cosθs​)​​

where θs\theta_sθs​ is the polar angle measured from the ecliptic North Pole, and ϕs\phi_sϕs​ is the ecliptic longitude.

Thus,

Δt(t)=R(cos⁡ϕ(t),sin⁡ϕ(t),0)⋅(sin⁡θscos⁡ϕs,sin⁡θssin⁡ϕs,cos⁡θs)=Rsin⁡θs[cos⁡ϕ(t)cos⁡ϕs+sin⁡ϕ(t)sin⁡ϕs]=Rsin⁡θscos⁡[ϕ(t)−ϕs]\begin{align} \Delta t(t) &= R (\cos\phi(t), \sin\phi(t), 0) \cdot (\sin \theta_s \cos\phi_s, \sin\theta_s \sin \phi_s, \cos\theta_s) \\ &= R \sin \theta_s [\cos \phi(t) \cos \phi_s + \sin \phi(t) \sin\phi_s] \\ &= R \sin \theta_s \cos[\phi(t) - \phi_s] \end{align} Δt(t)​=R(cosϕ(t),sinϕ(t),0)⋅(sinθs​cosϕs​,sinθs​sinϕs​,cosθs​)=Rsinθs​[cosϕ(t)cosϕs​+sinϕ(t)sinϕs​]=Rsinθs​cos[ϕ(t)−ϕs​]​​

Then,

ψD=2πf0Rsin⁡θscos⁡[ϕ(t)−ϕs]\begin{equation} \psi_D = 2\pi f_0 R \sin \theta_s \cos[\phi(t) - \phi_s] \end{equation} ψD​=2πf0​Rsinθs​cos[ϕ(t)−ϕs​]​​

Obviously, when θs=0\theta_s = 0θs​=0, the Doppler effect vanishes, whereas when θs=π/2\theta_s = \pi/2θs​=π/2, the Doppler effect reaches its maximum.

6 Two-body extreme mass ratio inspirals

The EMRI systems emit important GW all the time, but the most important bursts of energy occur at periapsis. After every circle around the orbit, the semi-axis decays a fraction proportional to the energy loss. After typically 104−10510^4 - 10^5104−105 cycles, the small body plunges through the horizon of the MBH and is lost. The emission of GW finishes.

FastEMRIWaveforms (FEW) package

This framework allows gravitational selfforce-based waveform models to be computed about as rapidly as kludge models, while retaining their inherent accuracy. The key observation in our approach is that only the waveform phase needs to be known to very high precision; the amplitudes of the thousands of harmonic modes in an EMRI can computed to a much lower accuracy.
